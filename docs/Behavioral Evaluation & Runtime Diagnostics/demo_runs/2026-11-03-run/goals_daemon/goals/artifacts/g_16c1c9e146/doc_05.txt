Early example of Chinese writing, the Oracle bone script The Chinese language is a Sino-Tibetan language which originated in the vicinity of the Yellow River Valley . The earliest historical linguistic evidence of the spoken Chinese language dates back approximately 3,500 years, [ 1 ] while examples of the writing system that would become written Chinese are attested in inscriptions made on bronze vessels and oracle bones during the Late Shang period ( c. 1250 – 1050 BCE), [ 2 ] [ 3 ] with the very oldest dated to c. 1250 BCE . [ 4 ] In the first millennium BCE and first millennium CE, the language spread beyond the Yellow River basin, primarily into what is now Southern China , gradually replacing the indigenous languages of the region. With the end of the Han dynasty (202 BCE - 220 CE) , Old Chinese developed into Middle Chinese . Successive waves of migration brought overlapping layers of linguistic influence to the south, while northern speech continued to change over the centuries, diverging into today's Chinese varieties . [ 5 ] One major development in Northern China was the emergence of Old Mandarin in the 11th or 12th century, which contained a number of distinctive innovations setting it apart from older forms of Chinese. [ 6 ] This new form of northern speech spread rapidly throughout northern China and subsequently encroached into parts of southern China as well, internally diverging in the process. Various southern Chinese varieties later spread to other parts of the world with the Chinese diaspora , while northern varieties simultaneously spread to the peripheries of China's northern territories in the Zou Xikou and Chuang Guandong migratory movements. Although the varieties of Chinese lie within a dialect continuum , the Chinese language historically relied on standard speech forms such as yayan to bridge the low or non-existing mutual intelligibility between more distant forms. [ 7 ] The term "Mandarin" first emerged to describe the Nanjing -based lingua franca of officials in Late Imperial China . In the mid-19th century, the standard form of Mandarin shifted from the earlier Nanjing-based Mandarin to a different form of Mandarin local to Beijing . [ 8 ] [ 9 ] This Beijing-based speech subsequently became known in the 20th century as Modern Standard Chinese . [ 10 ] Until recent times, Written Chinese was dominated by Classical, or Literary Chinese , a system of syntax and vocabulary based on the written language of ancient Chinese Classics before 221 BCE. Classical Chinese served as the language of formal writing and a lingua franca across East Asia until the end of the 19th century. [ 11 ] However, in the Ming dynasty (1368-1644) , Chinese writers started using baihua ( Written vernacular Chinese ) to write fiction, [ 12 ] such as the Classic Chinese novels . Nevertheless, it was only in the early 20th century New Culture Movement that baihua displaced the dominance of Classical Chinese. At the same time, there were growing reformist calls to simplify Chinese characters , with the Chinese Nationalist government first attempting to simplify them in 1935, [ 13 ] followed by a more extensive effort by the communist People's Republic of China in 1956. [ 15 ] The oldest attested written Chinese —comprising the oracle bone inscriptions made during the 13th century BCE by the Shang dynasty royal house in modern Anyang , Henan—is also the earliest direct evidence of the Sinitic languages . Most experts agree that Sinitic languages share a common ancestor with the Tibeto-Burman languages , forming the primary Sino-Tibetan family. However, the precise placement of Sinitic within Sino-Tibetan is a matter of debate. [ 16 ] Reconstructing the common ancestor of all Sino-Tibetan languages, known as Proto-Sino-Tibetan —a language last spoken thousands of years before the historical record—has been challenging. Even though Chinese characters are logographs that do not encode the sounds of speech directly, written Chinese allows for the partial reconstruction of how ancient forms of the language sounded. No such evidence exists for earlier stages, including where Sinitic would have split from other branches of Sino-Tibetan. [ 17 ] There are several reasons that the comparative method conventionally used in historical linguistics has had limited success in reconstructing the history of Sino-Tibetan compared to the results achieved with other major language families like Indo-European : [ 18 ] Relatively few Sino-Tibetan languages, of which Chinese is one, have written traditions old enough to provide useful comparative evidence. In areas where Sino-Tibetan languages are spoken, there have historically been high rates of population transfer. This has resulted in many areal features shared between languages, making it difficult to determine which relationships—not only cognates in vocabulary, but also grammatical features—are phylogenetic . Languages that make heavier use of morphology generally provide more data useful in historical comparisons, i.e. the conjugated and derived forms of words. Compared to Indo-European languages like Latin, Greek, and Sanskrit, Sino-Tibetan languages are highly isolating and exhibit very little morphology, making it difficult to identify cognates. Linguists Alexander Vovin and George van Driem suggest that a number of proposed cognates between Austronesian and Sinitic languages indicate there was an Austronesian substratum in Sinitic, which may date back as far as the third or fourth millennia BCE in the Longshan interaction sphere. [ 19 ] [ 20 ] [ 21 ] Pre-Classical period (1300–500 BCE) [ edit ] Chinese inscription on bronze wine vessel in the shape of a horse , Western Zhou dynasty Old Chinese , sometimes known as "Archaic Chinese", is ancestral to all current Chinese languages. The first known use of the Chinese writing system is divinatory inscriptions into tortoise shells and oracle bones during the Shang dynasty (1766–1122 BCE). During the first half of the Zhou dynasty (1122–256 BCE), writing descended from the Shang is found in texts including inscriptions on bronze artefacts, the Classic of Poetry and the history of the Book of Documents . [ 22 ] Phonetic elements found in the majority of Chinese characters also provide hints to their Old Chinese pronunciations, as do the pronunciations of borrowed Chinese characters in Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese. [ 23 ] Old Chinese was not wholly uninflected . It possessed a rich sound system in which aspiration or rough breathing differentiated the consonants, but was probably still without tones. Work on reconstructing Old Chinese started with Qing dynasty philologists. [ 24 ] Classical period (500 BCE – 1 CE) [ edit ] Main article: Old Chinese phonology Words in Old Chinese were generally monosyllabic ; as such, each character denoted an independent word. [ 25 ] Affixes could be added to form a new word, which was often written with the same single character. In many cases, the pronunciations then diverged due to the systematic sound changes caused by the affixes. For example, many modern readings reflect the departing tone present in Middle Chinese , which many scholars now believe is a reflex of a derivational suffix /*-s/ that served a range of semantic functions in Old Chinese.
