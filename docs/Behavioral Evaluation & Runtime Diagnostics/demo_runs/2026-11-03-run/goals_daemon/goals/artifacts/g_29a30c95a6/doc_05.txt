Find sources: "History of autism" – news · newspapers · books · scholar · JSTOR ( October 2026 ) ( Learn how and when to remove this message ) ( Learn how and when to remove this message ) The history of autism encompasses various conceptual and treatment approaches, with the understanding of autism having been shaped by cultural, scientific, and societal factors. [ 1 ] Pathologized or viewed as beneficial as part of neurodiversity , autism has been subject to various treatments (e.g. electroconvulsive therapy) [ 2 ] [ 3 ] Progress in scientific understanding of autism has contributed to both treatment and perceptions of autism. [ 1 ] The term autism was first introduced by Eugen Bleuler in his description of schizophrenia in 1911. [ 4 ] The diagnosis of schizophrenia was broader than its modern equivalent; autistic children were often diagnosed with childhood schizophrenia . [ 5 ] The earliest research that focused on children who would today be considered autistic was conducted by Grunya Sukhareva starting in the 1920s. [ 6 ] In the 1930s and 1940s, Hans Asperger and Leo Kanner described two related syndromes, later termed Asperger syndrome and infantile autism . Kanner thought that the condition he had described might be distinct from schizophrenia, [ 5 ] [ 4 ] and in the following decades, research into what would become known as autism accelerated. [ 4 ] Formally, however, autistic children continued to be diagnosed under various terms related to schizophrenia in both the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) and International Classification of Diseases (ICD), [ 5 ] but by the early 1970s, it had become more widely recognized that autism and schizophrenia were in fact distinct disorders, [ 5 ] and in 1980, this was formalized for the first time with new diagnostic categories in the DSM-III . [ 7 ] Asperger syndrome was introduced to the DSM as a formal diagnosis in 1994, but in 2013, Asperger syndrome and infantile autism were reunified into a single diagnostic category, autism spectrum disorder (ASD). [ 7 ] Autistic individuals often struggle with understanding non-verbal social cues and emotional sharing. The development of the web has given many autistic people a way to form online communities , work remotely, and attend school remotely which can directly benefit those experiencing difficulty communicating typically. Societal and cultural aspects of autism have developed: some in the community seek a cure, while others believe that autism is simply another way of being. [ 8 ] [ 9 ] [ 10 ] Although the rise of organizations and charities relating to advocacy for autistic people and their caregivers and efforts to destigmatize ASD have affected how ASD is viewed, [ 11 ] autistic individuals and their caregivers continue to experience social stigma in situations where autistic peoples' behaviour is thought of negatively, [ 12 ] and many primary care physicians and medical specialists express beliefs consistent with outdated autism research. [ 13 ] The discussion of autism has brought about much controversy. Without researchers being able to meet a consensus on the varying forms of the condition, there was for a time a lack of research being conducted on what is now classed as autism. [ 14 ] [ 15 ] [ 16 ] Discussing the syndrome and its complexity frustrated researchers. Controversies have surrounded various claims regarding the etiology of autism. [ citation needed ] Autism before the term "autism" (until 1908) [ edit ] In European folklore [ edit ] A devil swaps a child for a changeling in this 1862 illustration by Gustave Doré . Scholars have suggested that stories of changelings in European folklore may have originated as a folk explanation for autism . The tales involve young children being taken by fairies or other beings, and replaced by changelings. These children were "unresponsive, resistant to affection, did not express emotion, cried a lot or did not speak." Autism may not be initially apparent when a child is very young but become more clear over time, which contributed to the myth that a child had been replaced. [ 17 ] The connection between changeling tales and autism was first proposed in 2005 by Australian social scientist Julie Leask . She was prompted into the line of inquiry after hearing a modern mother claim her true child had been "stolen". [ 18 ] Autistic people before "autism" [ edit ] Authors have identified cases, from long before the term "autism" was created, of people who perhaps today would have been diagnosed as autistic. The Table Talk of Martin Luther , compiled by his note taker, Mathesius, contains the story of a 12-year-old boy who may have been autistic with high support needs. [ 19 ] The earliest well-documented case of autism is that of Hugh Blair of Borgue , as detailed in a 1747 court case in which his brother successfully petitioned to annul Blair's marriage to gain Blair's inheritance. [ 20 ] Henry Cavendish was a prolific natural philosopher , first published in 1766. During his life, Cavendish was considered eccentric and his behaviour was described as "peculiarly shy" by contemporaries. When researching Cavendish as a subject for a 2001 article in the journal Neurology , neurologist Oliver Sacks determined that evidence for an ASD diagnosis was "almost overwhelming". [ 21 ] [ 22 ] [ 23 ] Early descriptions of autistic behaviors [ edit ] Aphasia voluntaria was defined in 1877 by German doctor Adolf Kussmaul as choosing not to speak. [ 24 ] Developmental retardation was a term British doctor John Down used in 1877 to describe conditions including what would be considered autism today. [ 25 ] Echolalia is the practice of repeating other people's words without explicit awareness. French doctor Jean Marc Gaspard Itard coined the term in 1825. [ 26 ] [ 27 ] Interoception and proprioception were concepts introduced in 1906 by British neurophysiologist Charles Sherrington . [ 28 ] [ 29 ] Interoception describes the sensory system that receives internal information from the body. Autistic people's minds are sometimes less effective at receiving this information than those of non-autistic people, particularly during childhood or when experiencing sensory overload. [ 30 ] [ 31 ] Sometimes interoceptive signals in autistic people are stronger than is typical in non-autistic people. [ 32 ] Proprioception describes the system that senses the position and location of muscles, tendons and joints. Autistic people's minds can be less effective at receiving this information than those of non-autistic people. [ 33 ] Monomania was defined around 1810 by French psychiatrist Jean-Étienne Dominique Esquirol , who published about it in 1827. [ 34 ] It was centred on the contemporary concept of the fixed idea , or idée fixe , a single subject of obsession in an otherwise healthy mind. [ 34 ] [ 35 ] Obsessional thought was explored by Austrian psychiatrist Richard von Krafft-Ebing in a pioneering 1867 work. [ 36 ] [ 37 ] Obsessive neurosis was a term first used by Austrian psychiatrist Sigmund Freud in 1894. [ 36 ] [ 38 ] Palilalia was initially defined as auto-echolalia by French doctor Édouard Brissaud in 1899. [ 39 ] Psychasthenia was a newly defined condition included in French psychiatrist Pierre Janet 's 1903 book Les Obsessions et la Psychasthénie , meaning The obsessions and psychasthenia . [ citation needed ] Soviet psychiatrist Grunya Sukhareva believed it to be a component of schizoid psychopathy in childhood. [ 40 ] Savant syndrome was described by John Down in an 1887 lecture. He used the term idiots savants for people whose mental abilities were generally poor but who had strong abilities in a particular area. [ 41 ] Sensory overload in urban environments was discussed by German sociologist Georg Simmel in his 1903 essay " The Metropolis and Mental Life ". [ 42 ] Stereotypy was first defined by French psychiatrist Jean-Pierre Falret in 1864. [ 43 ] [ 44 ] Dementia praecox and related conditions [ edit ] The term dementia praecox (premature dementia ) was first used by German psychiatrist Heinrich Schüle in 1880, [ 45 ] and also by 1891 by Arnold Pick , a Czech professor of psychiatry. [ 46 ] Scottish psychiatrist Thomas Clouston in his 1883 book Clinical Lectures on Mental Diseases , described a new condition he called psychoneurosis . [ 47 ] The term "dementia praecox" was greatly popularised in 1899 through the sixth edition of German Psychiatrist Emil Kraepelin 's book Psychiatrie. Ein Lehrbuch für Studirende und Aerzte [ 48 ] (Psychiatry. A text-book for students and physicians). [ 49 ] [ 50 ] This condition was defined very broadly by today's standards. The primary disturbance in dementia praecox was seen to be a disruption in cognitive or mental functioning in attention, memory, and goal-directed behaviour. [ 51 ] Italian psychiatrist Sante De Sanctis briefly mentioned a condition in a 1906 paper [ 52 ] [ 53 ] he called dementia praecocissima (very premature dementia), which was a form of dementia praecox that started very early in people's lives. He wrote about it in more detail in a 1908 paper. [ 54 ] Austrian educator Theodor Heller defined a condition called dementia infantilis (infantile dementia) in 1908. [ 55 ] Autism as a symptom of schizophrenia (1908–1924) [ edit ] Eugen Bleuler [ edit ] Eugen Bleuler created the concept of autism , using it to describe a type of behaviour. Swiss psychiatrist Eugen Bleuler gave a lecture explaining that dementia praecox was very different to other forms of dementia in April 1908. [ 56 ] He proposed that it be given the unique name schizophrenia - a split mind. [ citation needed ] What is now known as "schizophrenia" is different from what Bleuler described. He included what is today considered as autism, schizoid personality disorder and various schizophrenia spectrum and other psychotic disorders in his definition. [ citation needed ] Bleuler coined the word autismus (English translation autism ) in July 1910. [ 57 ] He derived autismus from the Greek word: αὐτός , romanized : autós , lit. ' self ' , and used it to mean morbid self-admiration, referring to "autistic withdrawal of the patient to his fantasies, against which any influence from outside becomes an intolerable disturbance". [ 57 ] [ 58 ] He first used it in print to describe a symptom of schizophrenia in the scientific paper "Zur Theorie des schizophrenen Negativismus" [ 58 ] (On the theory of schizophrenic negativism). Bleuler believed that the idiosyncratic behaviours of people displaying autistic behaviour were due to engaging with personal fantasy rather than with the world as it is. [ 4 ] He believed they drew on an early childhood mental state that was unable to form theory of mind . [ 4 ] August Hoch defined the shut-in character type. August Hoch: the shut-in personality [ edit ] Swiss-American psychiatrist August Hoch defined the concept of the shut-in personality. In papers published in 1909 and 1910, he described it as including reticence, seclusiveness, shyness and a preference for living in fantasy worlds. [ 59 ] [ 60 ] [ 61 ] They might also have difficulties with romantic and sexual relationships. [ 62 ] According to Hoch, a high proportion of patients with dementia praecox had shut-in behaviour prior to developing more serious symptoms. [ 60 ] Gannushkin and Kraepelin [ edit ] Emil Kraepelin defined the verschrobene (eccentric) character type. Psychiatrists Pyotr Gannushkin and Emil Kraepelin [ 63 ] detailed character types that would later be considered schizoid by Grunya Sukhareva . [ 64 ] Kraepelin described patients who, despite their intelligence, could show traits inconsistent with their mental abilities. They could be forgetful, absent-minded and show variable intellectual capacity. [ 63 ] They might also be extravagant in their thinking, communicate in a rambling or confusing manner, and occupy themselves with fruitless plans. They might also have difficulty connecting with others. [ 63 ] Introduction of the term schizoid [ edit ] In 1924, psychiatrist Ernst Kretschmer described schizoid people as socially withdrawn, suspicious of others, and lacking in conversational abilities. They are at the same time complacent and sensitive. Their interests may be narrow, vague, or grandiose. [ 59 ] [ 65 ] Bleuler also believed that everyone had a schizoid element, to a greater or lesser degree. [ 65 ] Carl Jung: Introversion [ edit ] Carl Jung coined the term introverted . Main article: Introversion § History In September 1909, Swiss psychiatrist Carl Jung used the term introverted in a lecture at Clark University to describe love turned inward, increasing imaginative activity. [ 66 ] [ 67 ] The lecture was published in 1910, which was the term’s first appearance in print. [ 67 ] A more concise definition of the introverted type was given by Jung in February 1936, in his paper "Psychologische Typologie" [ 68 ] (Psychological Typology). It included: He holds aloof from external happenings, does not join in, has a distinct dislike of society as soon as he finds himself among too many people. In a large gathering he feels lonely and lost. The more crowded it is, the greater becomes his resistance. He is not in the least "with it", and has no love of enthusiastic get-togethers. He is not a good mixer. What he does, he does in his own way, barricading himself against influences from outside. He is apt to appear awkward, often seeming inhibited, and it frequently happens that, by a certain brusqueness of manner, or by his glum unapproachability, or some kind of malapropism, he causes unwitting offence to people... Hans Asperger would later see similarities between Jung's introversion and his concept of autism. [ 69 ] Carl Jung's 1921 book Psychologische Typen [ 70 ] was published as Personality Types [ 71 ] in English in 1923. The International Council for the Education of Exceptional Children [ edit ] American teacher Elizabeth Farrell founded the International Council for the Education of Exceptional Children on August 10, 1922 to bring together teachers of disabled children. [ 72 ] The group later became the Council for Exceptional Children. [ 73 ] Moritz Tramer [ edit ] In 1924, psychiatrist Moritz Tramer published a paper describing described idiot savants , "Einseitig Talentierte und Begabte Schwachsinnige" (Singularly talented and gifted mental defectives). [ 74 ] [ 75 ] Pioneering research (1925–1945) [ edit ] Grunya Sukhareva [ edit ] Grunya Sukhareva described children who would now be considered autistic. Soviet child psychiatrist Grunya Sukhareva was the first to publish a comprehensive clinical description of children who would now be considered autistic. [ 76 ] In 1921, she founded a school for children with psychological problems at the Psychoneurological Department for Children in Moscow. [ 76 ] In 1925, she published a paper offering a detailed description of what she called schizoid psychopathies in children, along with six case studies of boys. (A German version appeared in 1926. [ 76 ] ) Her description closely resembled the diagnostic criteria for autism spectrum disorder in the DSM-5 . [ 6 ] According to Sukhareva, the children generally remained apart from their peers and had difficulty adapting to social environments. They showed a tendency towards abstract and schematic thinking, which made them appear eccentric to their classmates. She described a preference for fantastical stories and fairy tales, avoidance of group play, and a tendency towards solitude from early childhood. Loneliness and social isolation were prevalent. Several children experienced ridicule or name-calling from other children, such as being called "talking machines." [ 40 ] Several children displayed what she called flatness and superficiality of feelings. She also noted limited facial expressions and expressive movements. [ 40 ] Sukhareva noted a tendency to stick to work that had been started, a kind of rigidity of thinking and difficulty adapting to the new. Four of the six children presented with what she described as a silly demeanour, including a tendency to rhyme and create stereotypical new words. She also noted linguistic peculiarities and difficulties with movement. [ 40 ] Sukhareva distinguished the condition from schizophrenia, arguing that the two shared some clinical features but differed profoundly in their underlying development. She nevertheless proposed that the condition involved an inborn deficiency in systems also affected in schizophrenia. [ 40 ] In 1927, she published a paper focusing on five girls with the condition and describing differences between boys and girls. [ 77 ] One of her patients "strives to mask her embarrassment with laughter, grimaces, and superfluous movements." [ 78 ] In 1930, Sukhareva published a paper describing difficulties with movement, emotional responses and thinking. [ 79 ] Between 1932 and 1936, Sukhareva went on to publish several papers about childhood schizophrenia. [ 76 ] Her work was read and cited by other researchers, which included Louise Despert, Charles Bradley and Leo Kanner . However, she remained little known outside Russia. [ 76 ] Hans Asperger [ edit ] Hans Asperger was the first to publish the name autism for a specific condition, and the first to document many of that condition's attributes. [ citation needed ] In 1929, German psychiatrist Erich Rudolf Jaensch [ de ] published Grundformen menschlichen Seins (Basic forms of human existence). [ 80 ] Asperger would later say his autism thinking was influenced by its explanation of schizothyms . [ 69 ] In April 1935, Anni Weiss published the paper "Qualitative intelligence testing as a means of diagnosis in the examination of psychopathic children", [ 81 ] which includes a case study about an autistic boy. [ 82 ] In 1934, Frankl published the paper "Befehlen und Gehorchen" [ 83 ] [ 84 ] (Command and Obey), which identified a group of children with language difficulties that some have subsequently considered autistic. [ 85 ] Frankl left Vienna in 1937 [ 82 ] and went to work with his friend Leo Kanner at Johns Hopkins Hospital. [ 86 ] Asperger used the terms autistic psychopath and autism in a 3 October 1938 lecture [ 87 ] to describe a pattern he had seen in his patients and elsewhere. [ 88 ] The lecture included two case studies, and analysis. It instructed its predominantly Viennese listeners and readers that people who are a bit strange may also be very intelligent, and that knowing this will become important "when the ' Law for the Prevention of Hereditary Diseased Offspring ' comes into force in our country." [ 88 ] Asperger submitted a postdoctoral habilitation thesis on the topic of autism to the University of Vienna in October 1942, [ 76 ] which would be published with very few changes in June 1944. [ 89 ] This paper "Die "Autistischen Psychopathen" im Kindesalter" (The "Autistic Psychopaths" in Childhood) [ 69 ] included four cases studies and related analysis over 61 pages.
