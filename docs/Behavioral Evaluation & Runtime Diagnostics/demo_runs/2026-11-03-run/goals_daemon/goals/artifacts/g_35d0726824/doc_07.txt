Illusionism (philosophy of mind) - Wikipedia Jump to content From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia View in philosophy of mind Illusionism is a family of views in philosophy of mind according to which phenomenal consciousness is an illusion that either does not exist or is a misrepresentation of real neural processes. Closely related to eliminative materialism , it is generally a form of materialism or physicalism as it argues that consciousness is determined entirely by the physical world. It is also related to Michael Graziano 's attention schema theory , which considers the brain to be an information processing device that gathers information about its own attention into an internal model of reality which it then represents as a conscious experience, mistakenly ascribing to it special attributes it does not actually have. Illusionism argues that qualia —the phenomenal properties of sensations, such as "what it is like" to experience color or sound—are poorly defined or lack an empirical foundation, and should be rejected or redefined. Proponents of illusionism include philosophers Daniel Dennett , Keith Frankish and Jay L. Garfield , as well as Graziano. The term was coined in 2016 by Frankish, who wrote that illusionism does not deny the existence of consciousness but offers a different account of what it is. Forms of strong illusionism posit that neither phenomenal consciousness nor qualia exist, standing in direct opposition to phenomenal realism, which asserts that they do. Forms of weak illusionism posit that they do exist but are misrepresented. This article refers to strong illusionism simply as illusionism . While the hard problem of consciousness asks why conscious experiences are accompanied by phenomenal properties, illusionism rejects the hard problem entirely, replacing it with the "illusion problem", which asks: "Why does it seem that experiences have phenomenal properties and why is this illusion so powerful?" Illusionism is a form of type-A materialism as it asserts that consciousness can be fully explained by solving the easy problems , which concern the mechanistic and behavioral basis of cognitive processes. Illusionism is sometimes called illusionism about consciousness , distinguishing it from similarly-named theories in other fields, such as illusionism in free will . [ 1 ] [ 2 ] Views that reject the existence of phenomenal consciousness have been criticized by philosophers David Chalmers , Galen Strawson , Jesse Prinz , John Searle and others. Critics argue that phenomenal experiences are obviously real, or find illusionism to be self-refuting. Overview [ edit ] To introduce illusionism in his 2016 paper "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness", Keith Frankish presents a thought experiment involving psychokinesis , the psychic ability to manipulate objects using the mind without physical interaction. According to Frankish, if such a radically inexplicable ability were one day demonstrated, science would have three options: Accept the phenomenon as real, possibly leading to major revisions to existing scientific theory. Accept the phenomenon as real, but argue that it is not anomalous and can be explained by existing scientific theory. Argue that the phenomenon is illusory, and investigate how this illusion is produced. [ 3.1 ] The first two options are realist – the first radical and the second conservative – proposing that the phenomenon is real, while the third option corresponds to illusionism. Frankish states that phenomenal properties also seem anomalous as they are inaccessible to third-person science and inexplicable in physical terms. We are infallible about them: we cannot be wrong about them. They are "private": no one can directly access anyone else's qualia. They are ineffable : the idea of them cannot be described with words. They are "intrinsic" and "simple" or impossible to analyze. [ 4 ] Dennett presents a series of thought experiments to demonstrate that qualia do not have these attributes. For example, the "inexperienced beer drinker": someone who drinks beer for the first time often finds the taste unpleasant, yet comes to enjoy it, although the composition of the taste remains the same. In response to the inverted spectrum scenario in which a neurosurgeon changes a patient's perception of color, for example causing them to perceive green as red, Dennett argues that instead of changing the perception, the patient's memory of the colors could be altered instead, leading to an identical reaction from them, concluding that we are not infallible about qualia. [ 4 ] Dennett also presented the phenomenon of change blindness as an example of this. [ 5 ] [ 3.2 ] In countering the knowledge argument , he argued that qualia are so poorly defined that asserting their existence does not serve empirical research as they introduce unanswerable questions, and should be rejected altogether. [ 6.1 ] [ 4 ] Dennett denied the conceivability of philosophical zombies —hypothetical entities that behave exactly like humans but lack phenomenal consciousness—by presenting a similar entity that thinks it is conscious, thinks it has qualia and thinks it suffers pains, but is wrong about these perceptions "in ways that neither they nor we could ever discover". [ 7 ] This led him to conclude that "we are all philosophical zombies" and reject the concept. [ 6.2 ] [ 8 ] David Chalmers, a critic of illusionism, presented a coincidence argument in its favor: if there exists a method that can explain our intuitions about phenomenal consciousness independently of the concepts of consciousness itself, and if those intuitions themselves are correct, then this correctness must be a coincidence. This is highly unlikely; therefore it is likely that our intuitions are not correct. [ 9.1 ] [ 10 ] Even if one dismisses this coincidence by assuming the existence of a causal link or psychophysical laws that guarantee this correlation, he finds it difficult to deny for several reasons: It is an odd coincidence that we are not philosophical zombies or in an inverted world where, for example, we feel pleasure when we should feel pain. It is an odd coincidence that our perception that phenomenal consciousness exists is, in turn, caused by consciousness and not by some other mechanism. [ 9.1 ] However, Chalmers rejects illusionism on the basis of the Moorean argument below . Strong and weak illusionism [ edit ] In his 2016 paper, Frankish distinguishes between the positions of strong and weak illusionism: weak illusionism argues that phenomenal properties are real but their nature is misrepresented, while strong illusionism argues that they do not exist at all. He highlights the difference between them by introducing the concept of a "quasi-phenomenal property" — a non-phenomenal, physical property that introspection misrepresents as phenomenal. Strong illusionists hold that introspectable qualities of subjective experience are merely quasi-phenomenal ones, while weak illusionists do not. [ 3.3 ] Weak theories posit the existence of what Frankish calls diet qualia— phenomenal characters of experience, but lacking the "special attributes" listed above. [ 3.3 ] [ 11 ] Finding it difficult to explain the existence of such properties in a physicalist framework, Frankish doubts the viability of weak illusionism, arguing that the position will either collapse into radical phenomenal realism—the view that phenomenal properties are real and will require serious revisions to scientific theory—or into strong illusionism.
