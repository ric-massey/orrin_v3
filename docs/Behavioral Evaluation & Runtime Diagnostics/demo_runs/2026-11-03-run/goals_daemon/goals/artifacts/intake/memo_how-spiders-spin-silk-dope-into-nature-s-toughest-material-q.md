A marbled orb weaver spider ( Araneus marmoreus ) builds its web one silk stitch at a time. Konrad Wothe/NaturePL/Science Source Introduction A spider, when knocked off a wall, doesn’t fall for long. Within milliseconds, it spins a soup of proteins into sturdy climbing gear. Using a thin, shimmering strand of silk, the spider hauls itself back up. The strand is tougher than Kevlar, the synthetic fiber in bike tires and bulletproof vests, and stronger than steel. It’s flexible and biocompatible with human bodies. Scientists dream of making spider silk into high-performing materials for seat belts, helmets, naval materials, and even scaffolds to help repair brain cells. But attempts to make spider silk in the lab have fallen short because researchers don’t know enough about how a spider spins “silk dope,” a liquid blend of proteins, water, and salt, from its silk glands into a remarkable solid. Mapping out the molecular mechanisms behind the process is “really the holy grail” for the field, said Gregory Holland , a chemist and spectroscopist at San Diego State University. Recently, he led research that described how the molecules in liquid spider dope reorganize into semiliquid droplets called condensates before turning into solid silk fiber. The result is an atomic-level look into the spider’s silk factory. The findings are also a practical guide to the formation of condensates, which nature has used repeatedly to organize molecules for genetic processes and for the creation of impressively strong biological materials. “It’s becoming clear that [condensates are] a common strategy that’s being used across a range of different organisms,” said Matthew Harrington , a biological materials scientist at McGill University who was not involved in the study. By unpacking how nature uses condensates, scientists hope to re-create some of its most rugged materials. Silk Factory Spiders are incredible natural factories. They can spin up to seven types of silks, and the same spider can often spin several of them. Aciniform silk wraps up insects for later snacking.
